## S1 — Cold open

### B01 — The $12,000 sentence
est: 0:45
type: hook
must_cover: true
Open on the story of a candidate who answered "what are you looking for?" with a
number, and left twelve thousand dollars on the table in a single sentence. Do not
explain the mechanism yet. End on the question: who should say the number first?

### B02 — What you'll learn
est: 1:00
type: roadmap
must_cover: true
Three outcomes for this lesson: you will be able to explain why anchoring works,
you will have four concrete levers for setting an anchor, and you will know how to
respond when someone anchors you first.

## S2 — The mechanism

### B03 — What anchoring actually is
est: 2:00
type: concept
must_cover: true
Define the anchoring effect: the first number in a negotiation disproportionately
shapes every number that follows, because we adjust from a starting point rather
than reasoning from scratch. Stress that this is an adjustment failure, not a
stupidity failure.

### B04 — The evidence
est: 2:30
type: evidence
must_cover: true
Cite the Northcraft and Neale real-estate study: professional appraisers were shown
identical properties with different listing prices, and their appraisals shifted by
41% between the low-anchor and high-anchor conditions. Emphasise that these were
trained experts who insisted the listing price had not influenced them.

### B05 — Why it works even when you know about it
est: 1:30
type: concept
Anchoring is not defeated by awareness. Knowing the effect exists reduces it a
little, but does not remove it. This is why process beats willpower.

## S3 — The four levers

### B06 — Lever 1: Precision
est: 2:00
type: framework
must_cover: true
A precise number reads as researched. $47,500 signals you did arithmetic; $50,000
signals you picked a round number. Precise anchors are also conceded against more
slowly.

### B07 — Lever 2: Range
est: 2:00
type: framework
must_cover: true
Bracketing: name a range whose bottom is your target. The other side hears the
bottom, but the range makes you sound flexible rather than rigid.

### B08 — Lever 3: Justification
est: 2:00
type: framework
must_cover: true
Attach a because-clause to the number. The reason does not have to be
overwhelming; an anchor with any justification is defended far better than a naked
number.

### B09 — Lever 4: Patience
est: 1:30
type: framework
must_cover: true
Say the number, then stop talking. The silence after an anchor is what makes it
land. Most people negotiate against themselves in the four seconds after they name
a price.

## S4 — Practice

### B10 — Role-play: the salary conversation
est: 3:00
type: demo
must_cover: true
Run the salary scenario live with the producer reading the hiring manager. Show a
weak anchor, then re-run the same moment with all four levers applied.

### B11 — Common mistakes
est: 2:00
type: pitfalls
Three mistakes: anchoring before you have information, anchoring inside your own
range instead of at its edge, and re-anchoring against yourself when the silence
gets uncomfortable.

### B12 — When not to anchor first
est: 1:30
type: concept
must_cover: true
Do not anchor first when you have materially worse information than the other
side. In that case ask questions until the information gap closes, and let them
put the first number on the table.
